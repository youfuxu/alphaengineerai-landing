Is this link safe? Check it before you click.

A familiar logo, padlock, short URL, or urgent message does not prove where a link leads. Inspect the destination without opening it, then verify the request through a known official route.

Check the message around the link →

A link can look normal and still be unsafe

Scammers use copied branding, shortened URLs, lookalike domains, redirects, fake delivery notices, account warnings, job offers, and payment pressure. The safest first move is to slow down before the link gets a chance to rush you.

Look at the real domain

Read the complete address, not only the visible brand name. Misspellings, extra words, unusual subdomains, lookalike characters, and unexpected country domains deserve independent verification.

Watch the request

A link that asks for a password, verification code, bank login, remote access, gift card, crypto payment, or urgent transfer is a reason to stop and use the official app or site instead.

Do not trust a green result alone

Reputation tools can miss new domains, and legitimate websites can be compromised. A result is context, not proof of identity, delivery, refund, employment, or investment claims.

Protect private links

Never share password-reset links, session URLs, private document links, one-time invitations, or addresses containing personal tokens with a public tool or another person.

A safer five-step link check

1. Do not open it from the message

If it arrived by SMS, email, DM, QR code, or an unexpected ad, leave it unopened. Find the service through a bookmark, the official app, or a domain you already know.

2. Copy the destination without following it

Use the message or browser's copy-link option. Inspect the full address as plain text. Do not click through a shortened URL just to see where it goes.

3. Compare the identity independently

Search for the organization separately, type its known domain yourself, and use a phone number or support route found outside the suspicious message. Never use contact details supplied by the link.

4. Use an official safety signal

You can check a domain with Google Safe Browsing site status. Treat a clean result as one signal, not a guarantee.

5. Review the message, not the private link

Remove names, phone numbers, codes, passwords, payment details, and private URLs, then use the global message checker guide to review the pressure pattern.

Stop rule: if a request still feels urgent or uncertain after you check it, do not sign in, pay, reply, download, or share a code. Verify through the official service first.

Common link traps

Account or bank warning

The message says your account will close, a payment failed, or suspicious activity was detected. Open the official app yourself and check there.

Delivery or customs fee

A small redelivery or customs charge can be used to collect card details. Check the shipment through the carrier's official app or typed domain.

Remote job or investment offer

Fake recruiters and investment promoters use links to move you to a copied portal, request a deposit, or demand an upfront fee. Verify the organization independently.

Family, friend, or support request

A familiar name or profile does not prove identity. Contact the person or provider through a known channel before sending money or private information.

If you already clicked

Do not panic and do not keep testing the page. From a trusted route, change any exposed password, contact your bank or payment provider if money or card details were involved, preserve evidence, and use the post-click recovery checklist. You can also find official reporting routes.
