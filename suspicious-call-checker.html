Is this phone call a scam? Pause before you call back.

A familiar caller ID, local number, company logo, or confident voice is not proof. Use this first check before you share a code, move money, install an app, or follow instructions.

What a suspicious call often tries to do

Scammers use pressure and borrowed identity to make a caller feel more trustworthy than the evidence supports.

Create an emergency

“Your account is being closed,” “your payment failed,” “your family member needs help,” or “you will be arrested” is designed to make you act before you verify.

Borrow a trusted identity

A caller may claim to be your bank, a government office, a delivery company, an employer, tech support, or someone you know. Caller ID and a familiar script can be copied.

Request a secret

Unexpected requests for a one-time code, password, PIN, full card details, identity document, or remote-access approval are stop signals.

Control the channel

They may tell you not to hang up, to use a number they provide, to download an app, or to keep the conversation secret from family, coworkers, or your bank.

Demand an unusual payment

Gift cards, cryptocurrency, wire transfers, cash hand-offs, payment apps, or fees to “protect” or “unlock” money deserve independent verification.

Exploit a callback

A missed call, voicemail, or text may push you to call an unfamiliar number. The safe move is to find the real organization or person yourself.

A safer five-minute call check

1. Slow the conversation

Say that you will verify independently and end the call. Do not stay on the line while opening your banking app, installing software, or moving money.

2. Treat caller ID as unverified

A displayed number can be spoofed. Do not call the number back from caller ID, a voicemail, or the caller’s message.

3. Use a known route

Find the number on the back of your card, a recent statement, the official app, an account bookmark, or the organization’s website typed by you. For a family emergency, call the person on a number you already have.

4. Check the request, not just the voice

Write down what was requested and review a redacted description with the global first-check guide. Remove names, numbers, codes, and private details first.

5. Report through the correct route

Block the number if appropriate, preserve the call time and messages, and use the official fraud or cybercrime route for your country. In the United Kingdom, Ofcom advises forwarding scam calls or messages to 7726; this route is not universal, so use your local official channel elsewhere.

Safe input rule: never paste passwords, payment details, verification codes, identity documents, private addresses, or a live session link into a public checker.

Official guidance and limits

United Kingdom

Everywhere

MindDividend Shield is a first-pass safety aid, not a caller-ID database, investigation, or guarantee. A clean lookup or familiar number cannot prove that a call is safe.

For country-by-country reporting options, use the official reporting directory.

If you already shared information or paid

Stop engaging. From a trusted device, change exposed passwords through the real service, contact your bank or payment provider immediately, ask about stopping or recalling the payment, preserve evidence, and report the incident. Ignore anyone who contacts you later promising guaranteed recovery for an upfront fee.
