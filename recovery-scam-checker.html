Recovery safety guide

Recovery scam checker: no real recovery requires a surprise payment.

After an online scam, a second person may claim to be a lawyer, investigator, bank employee, government official, tracing expert, or “recovery specialist.” Their goal is often to turn your first loss into another one.

This guide does not judge what happened. It helps you pause before sharing more information or paying someone who found you unexpectedly.

Warning signs of a second-chance scam

They contact you first

An unexpected call, direct message, email, comment, or “case referral” says they know about your loss and can get your money back.

There is an upfront fee

They ask for a retainer, tax, tracing fee, blockchain fee, release charge, gift card, crypto transfer, or payment before any result.

Official-looking identity

Badges, case numbers, copied logos, legal language, fake testimonials, and a polished website do not prove that the person represents a real organization.

More access or data is required

They request passwords, one-time codes, full bank details, identity documents, remote access, wallet seed phrases, or permission to move money “for verification.”

What to do before responding

Stop the second payment

Do not pay a new fee, send crypto, buy gift cards, move money to a “safe” account, or deposit a refund check and return the difference.

Verify from a clean starting point

Type the organization’s official web address yourself. Find its published contact route and ask whether the person, case, phone number, and payment request are real.

Protect accounts and evidence

Change exposed passwords from the official site, revoke remote access, contact your bank or payment provider, and save messages, headers, receipts, wallet addresses, and profile links.

Tell one trusted person

Scammers use embarrassment and urgency to isolate people. A second set of eyes can spot the pressure before another payment or disclosure.

Want a text-based second look? Remove names, account numbers, passwords, codes, wallet keys, and other private information, then use the MindDividend Shield checker to organize pressure and impersonation signals. It cannot verify a person or guarantee recovery.

If the offer came through a social platform or messaging app

Treat an unexpected direct message, comment, group invitation, or “support” account as an unverified lead—not as proof that the sender knows your case.

Save the public evidence

Keep the profile URL, username, message timestamps, payment instructions, receipts, and any case number. Redact private details before sharing evidence with anyone else.

Use the platform’s own report tools

After preserving evidence, report and block the account through the platform you opened independently. Do not keep replying just to test the story.

Warn people without reposting the scam

Share the public safety rule or a fictional, redacted example. Do not publish phone numbers, private screenshots, codes, wallet addresses, or a live payment link.

Questions people ask after a loss

Is a recovery fee ever safe?

Do not treat an unexpected recovery offer as trustworthy because it uses legal or technical language. Verify independently before sharing anything or paying.

What if they know my case details?

Scammers can buy lists, read public posts, impersonate support, or learn details from the first scam. Knowledge of your loss is not proof of legitimacy.

Should I confront them?

You do not need to argue. Stop responding, preserve evidence, secure accounts, and use official reporting and payment-provider channels.

Can I still try to recover the first loss?

Act quickly through your bank, card issuer, payment service, exchange, platform, and local official reporting route. Do not rely on a stranger who promises guaranteed results.
